From a photon wavepacket to a material excitation

A minimal microscopic transfer model · 3 October 2026

Finding: Absorption can transfer the relative amplitudes and phases of a photon into orthogonal material excitation channels. The apparatus specifies that transfer through its transition dipoles, orientation, mode overlap and frequency response. Direct channel recording preserves populations but does not expose their relative phase; coherent mixing before recording can expose it. This completes a specified minimal absorption-and-coherence model, with a phenomenological interface to records.

1. Physical system and boundaries

We choose one incoming photon, two orthogonal optical modes (for example polarizations), a material ground state |g⟩, and two orthogonal excited states |e₁⟩ and |e₂⟩. These may represent atomic transitions or idealized collective material modes. The two transitions couple to independent field channels, so there is no unmodeled cross-damping. Initially the matter is in |g⟩ and all unused field channels are in vacuum.

|1ψ⟩ = Σμ cμ ∫dt f(t) a†μ,in(t)|vac⟩ Σμ |cμ|² = 1; ∫dt |f(t)|² = 1.

f(t) is a photon probability amplitude, not a classical electric-field expectation. A one-photon state has zero mean electric field, while retaining measurable relative phases between modes or times. A single isolated two-level absorber left definitely excited carries no observable global phase. Two excitation channels, or an additional coherent reference, are needed to retain an accessible relative phase.

2. The interaction sets the apparatus response

Starting with the electric-dipole interaction −d·E and making the rotating-wave approximation gives a number-conserving exchange interaction:

Hᵢₙₜ/ℏ = Σjμ ∫dω [gⱼμ(ω)|eⱼ⟩⟨g| aμ(ω) + Hermitian conjugate].

The coupling g includes the transition dipole dotted into the optical polarization, spatial mode overlap and normalization. Thus selection rules and apparatus orientation enter before the final recording. A lossless analyzer can rotate incoming modes into the detector basis. For the example used here:

c′₁ = cosθ c₁ + sinθ c₂ c′₂ = −sinθ c₁ + cosθ c₂.

Here θ represents the full apparatus's mode orientation, not a spurious imbalance between output channels. The coupling matrix can include relative phases as well as this simple real rotation.

3. How the excitation is deposited

Eliminating a broadband field continuum in the Markov approximation, in the one-excitation sector, gives:

deⱼ/dt = −(Γⱼ/2 + iΔⱼ)eⱼ − i√(βⱼΓⱼ)c′ⱼ f(t) eⱼ(t) = −i√(βⱼΓⱼ)c′ⱼ ∫ₜ₀ᵗ ds exp[−(Γⱼ/2+iΔⱼ)(t−s)] f(s).

Γ is total radiative population decay, Δ is detuning, and β is the fraction of decay coupling to the addressed incident channel. All unused radiative channels account for loss. This is an amplitude equation for a quantized one-photon input, not a classical drive substituted into a saturated atom. In this sector it is linear. Pulse shape and losses in such excitation problems are treated by Rag and Gea-Banacloche; near-perfect mode-matched excitation is analyzed by Stobińska, Alber and Leuchs.

During interaction, the complete state contains both ground-state matter with a photon in the field and excited matter with field vacuum. It generally entangles matter and field. Tracing over the field gives:

ρmatter = (1−η)|g⟩⟨g| + Σij eᵢeⱼ* |eᵢ⟩⟨eⱼ| η = |e₁|² + |e₂|².

There is no ground-to-excited coherence in this reduced state, but there can be coherence between excited channels. This is the precise meaning of a deposited complex excitation in this model.

At a chosen capture time the transfer is v = A c, where A = diag(h₁,h₂)R(θ), and hⱼ is the pulse-response integral above. The excited block is Aρin A†. For matched channels, h₁=h₂, the normalized captured state preserves the entire input qubit in the apparatus basis. Unequal magnitudes filter the amplitudes; unequal phases rotate the relative phase. These effects are coherent distortions, not necessarily decoherence. Decoherence requires tracing over additional distinguishing information or stochastic averaging.

At the level of standard quantum measurement probabilities, the excitation channel has response operator Eⱼ=A†|j⟩⟨j|A. Thus this model constructs the apparatus contribution rather than simply declaring its preferred basis. Converting state weights into probabilities uses ordinary quantum mechanics; no new event-selection mechanism is proposed.

5. Relative-phase transfer and the sine difference

Your sine-difference intuition has a concrete microscopic counterpart. For a single resonant photon mode with amplitude b coupled to an excitation e with real coupling g:

de/dt = −ig b; db/dt = −ig e d|e|²/dt = 2g Im(e* b) = 2g |e||b| sin(arg b − arg e).

The relevant phase is a relative phase between joint-state amplitudes in a specified coupling convention. If g is complex, the invariant expression is 2 Im(e* g b). One cannot give physical significance to either amplitude phase by itself. The sine governs excitation current, including re-emission. A closed resonant exchange produces Rabi oscillations, not an attracting synchronized state. Phase locking would require further driven or dissipative dynamics and a demonstrable phase variable. The continuum model above also depends on phase through its response integral, without adding a Kuramoto law.

6. Extending the excitation to many constituents

If an excitation can be placed coherently at several material sites, its state can be |B⟩=Σn αn|eₙ⟩. In a short weak interaction, αn inherits the local optical mode amplitude and the complex dipole coupling. A spatial phase pattern can therefore become a collective excitation pattern. This assumes other degrees of freedom do not distinguish the sites; if they do, tracing them out suppresses the intersite coherence.

A single collective excitation distributed over many atoms is not yet an amplified macroscopic pointer. The coherent mapping is physically realizable: Tanji et al. demonstrated a heralded mapping of photon polarization into collective spin excitations, and Specht et al. mapped polarization states into and out of a single trapped atom. Those engineered memories show that absorption and loss of quantum phase information are distinct processes; they are not models of ordinary semiconductor detector cascades.

7. From excitation to records

For a controlled separation of stages, the numerical example assumes an ideal instantaneous coherent transfer at t=0 from each optical excited state to a long-lived storage state |sⱼ⟩. This is an explicit added control assumption, not a consequence of passive absorption. We then model dephasing and channel recording with a completely positive open-system evolution:

Lrecord,j = √k |Rⱼ⟩⟨sⱼ|, j=1,2 Lphase = √(γ/2)(|s₁⟩⟨s₁|−|s₂⟩⟨s₂|) ρ̇ = ΣL [LρL† − ½(L†Lρ+ρL†L)].

Separate record channels mean that the environment distinguishes the two records. R₁ and R₂ are absorbing labels, a coarse-grained interface rather than a microscopic amplifier. With equal k, their weights obey:

ρjj(t) = exp(−kt)ρjj(0) ρ12(t) = exp[−(k+γ)t]ρ12(0) recordⱼ(t) = [1−exp(−kt)]ρjj(0).

The stored excitation plus accumulated records is conserved. Pure dephasing reduces off-diagonal coherence without changing channel populations. The example uses k=0.6 and γ=0.8. Material-dependent phonons, electron scattering, carrier multiplication and the readout circuit are represented only through these effective terms; their microscopic rates are not derived here.

8. How a detector can reveal the transferred phase

Direct measurements of channels 1 and 2 access ρ11 and ρ22 and do not reveal ρ12. An alternative experiment delays recording, coherently mixes the stored channels and then records in a rotated basis. For |+δ⟩=(|s₁⟩+exp(iδ)|s₂⟩)/√2:

weight(+δ) = ½(ρ11+ρ22) + Re[exp(iδ)ρ12].

For equal input amplitudes with relative phase φ and matched capture η, after storage delay τ with recording switched off, this becomes η[1+exp(−γτ)cos(δ−φ)]/2. Our plotted fringe visibility is 1 at τ=0, 0.449 at τ=1 and 0.0907 at τ=3. Both the coherence decay and these values follow from the specified dephasing law. This is an alternative readout protocol to panel D, not simultaneous phase recovery after a destructive channel measurement. Repeated preparations and controlled analyzer settings reveal phase statistically.

9. What is complete

The model specifies the input state, microscopic exchange Hamiltonian, field-response approximation, apparatus orientation, material excitation amplitudes, surviving coherence, and an explicit open-system route to record weights. It explains where sine-dependent transfer enters and when a complex excitation can retain usable phase information. Its equations and numerical checks form a complete minimal model for that stated scope.

It is not a completed material-specific detector theory. Applying it to a photoelectric semiconductor or photon-counting CT requires the electronic continuum, outgoing-electron or electron–hole states, selection rules, phonon coupling, carrier transport and amplification. A phase-preserving memory is not automatically a phase-preserving counting detector. The outstanding physical question is which of those extra degrees of freedom retain or reveal which-channel information, and on what timescale. No general claim about their coherence lifetime is made here.
