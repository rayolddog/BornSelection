Time-dependent basins from complex detector excitations

Semiconductor photon-counting setting · Exploratory reduced model · 3 October 2026

Result: Incident-pulse variations, sampled complex surface fluctuations, and equilibrium thermal noise produce well-defined microscopic basins that change as the observation window grows. Their measured weights do not reproduce Born probabilities for the tested threshold mechanism. First-crossing competition strongly favors the larger incident amplitude. Independent readouts also produce many double crossings. These are results of the specified surrogate, not predictions for a calibrated semiconductor detector.

What was calculated

Eight model configurations, 21 nominal input populations and 4,000 independent histories per input were tested. Each history was evaluated at five observation gates (2, 4, 6, 8 and 12) and three thresholds (0.15, 0.35 and 0.70). No-signal controls, step-size checks and a relative-phase check are included. The same histories are reused across states and thresholds to reveal moving basin boundaries; these scans are correlated and must not be treated as independent evidence.

Unlike the previous deterministic absorption model, this calculation does sample complex surface-field variables and thermal trajectories. The vacuum part is a classical Gaussian surrogate for a specified passive-surface spectrum. It is not a sample of a complete interacting QED wavefunction, and it is not a material-specific quantum-vacuum calculation.

The user selected a semiconductor photon-counting detector. That architecture converts absorption into electron–hole excitations whose charge is collected by a bias field and processed into pulses. CdTe devices are a concrete example, described by Siemens Healthineers. The model below represents two competing collection channels. It does not use measured CdTe permittivity, electron band structure or charge-transport parameters; no temperature, wavelength, slit geometry, material or operating field was specified.

How the basins depend on time

Each column is the same microscopic history at each gate. Blue and orange indicate which channel crossed first; gray indicates no crossing yet. These are finite-observation basins: once a first record occurs, its label stays fixed. Time dependence here primarily means previously unresolved histories acquire a record, not that an existing record changes its identity.

For p=0.5, 38.8% of combined-model histories have no record by gate 4; all have a first record by gate 6 at threshold 0.35. At the larger threshold 0.70, 15.0% still have no record by gate 12. Discarding these misses can change the apparent outcome frequencies.

The complex excitation and selection rule

For each microscopic history λ we calculate two complex effective excitation coordinates:

zⱼ(t;λ) = c′ⱼ(λ) s(t;λ) + vⱼ(t;λ) + uⱼ(t;λ) ds/dt = −s/2 − i√0.8 fλ(t), s(0)=0. Qⱼ(t;λ) = ∫₀ᵗ |zⱼ(s;λ)|² ds. τⱼ(λ) = first time Qⱼ reaches qⱼ.

f is a normalized Gaussian photon-envelope amplitude of width 0.45, centered near time 3. v is the filtered surface surrogate and u the thermal coordinate. All units are dimensionless. Integrating |z|² represents an assumed detector response accumulating toward a threshold. It is not established here as actual deposited energy or collected semiconductor charge. There is no carrier depletion, transport or microscopic back-action from threshold crossing. Treating quantum amplitudes and random classical fields as additive complex coordinates is the semiclassical hypothesis being tested.

Two record rules are evaluated from the same trajectories:

  • Independent readouts: neither, only channel 1, only channel 2, or both cross threshold.
  • First-record competition: the earlier crossing wins, equivalent to an idealized latch that suppresses the later record. Ties and no-record cases remain explicit.

No comparison of a random uniform variable with |c₁|² is used. Outcome basins are sets B₁(T)={λ: τ₁<τ₂ and τ₁≤T}, with analogous sets for channel 2, ties and no records. Counts estimate their measures under the specified source, surface and thermal distributions. There is no fitting of those distributions to Born frequencies.

Incident photon variations and the slit

We use arrival jitter δt~N(0,0.18²) and small mode-orientation jitter δθ~N(0,0.05²). Each pulse is normalized to the same input norm; the mode amplitudes are transformed by a norm-preserving rotation. The principal scan uses c=(√p,√(1−p)); a supplementary scan changes the second component's relative phase to 0, π/2 and π.

A fixed slit does not by itself imply that each photon must be assigned a different classical ray or wavepacket. It can produce the same diffracted quantum mode on repeated preparations. In a fully specified experiment, the aperture and propagation should first determine that coherent mode. The sampled jitter here stands for source timing and alignment fluctuations, not a calculated diffraction-angle distribution. Sampling slit arrival positions from |ψ|² would insert the very probability rule under investigation, so this was not done.

How the surface constrains the vacuum surrogate

For a real material, macroscopic QED relates the field correlation tensor to the imaginary part of the electromagnetic Green tensor, which depends on surface geometry and complex permittivity. At zero temperature the positive-frequency vacuum correlation is proportional to ℏμ₀ω² Im G; temperature introduces occupation factors, and a classical surrogate must specify which ordering of quantum correlations it represents. See Scheel and Buhmann, Macroscopic QED.

Here a passive Lorentz dielectric supplies a computable, explicitly assumed scalar surface response:

ε(ω) = 1 + 2ω₀²/(ω₀²−ω²−i0.3ω) r(ω) = [ε(ω)−1]/[ε(ω)+1] S(ω) ∝ [0.02ω³ + Im r(ω)] exp(−ω/6), 0<ω≤12.

The ω³ term represents a free-space dipole-like spectral shape; Im r is inspired by the quasistatic reflected Green function near a planar surface. The actual distance and tensor prefactors are replaced by chosen dimensionless weights. ω₀=2 in the base case and 3 in a sensitivity case. S is normalized over the sampled modes; the independent complex Gaussian mode coefficients have E|A|²=1 and the drive scale is 0.12. Thus neither the absolute vacuum strength nor its ratio to a single photon has been derived from material electrodynamics.

We filter each mode with H(ω)=−i/[0.5+i(ω−2)], the response of the same damped effective excitation coordinate. A stationary field history is generated by Fourier synthesis with period 81.92; only the first 12 units are analyzed. Channel correlation is zero by default and 0.6 in a sensitivity test. This correlation parameter makes a positive covariance matrix but is not a computed spatial Green tensor.

The calculated filtered variance is 0.0018822 and the sampled variance is 0.0018698. This checks the stochastic sampler against its specified covariance. It does not validate the covariance as that of a particular semiconductor surface.

Thermal constraints and detector bias

The thermal coordinate is a complex Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process initialized in equilibrium, with correlation decay rate 0.5 and E|u|²=0.08². Its exact discrete update is:

u(t+Δt) = a u(t) + 0.08√(1−a²) ξ; a=exp(−0.5Δt) ξ is independent circular complex Gaussian, E|ξ|²=1.

This matches noise injection to relaxation so the intended harmonic-coordinate equilibrium variance is preserved. The measured variance is 0.0063880 versus 0.0064 prescribed. It is not a sampled many-electron thermal wavefunction or a Fermi–Dirac occupation calculation. Thermal carrier fluctuations in a semiconductor require its band structure, chemical potential, traps and temperature. A biased active detector need not be globally in thermal equilibrium, even if its lattice is approximately thermal. Equilibrium fluctuation relations cannot simply be assumed for every driven degree of freedom. See Seifert and Speck on nonequilibrium response.

The biased-threshold test uses q₁=0.88q and q₂=1.12q. It represents unequal readout sensitivity, not a calculated voltage effect or measurement-axis rotation. The principal case has equal thresholds. Apparatus mode orientation is accounted for separately.

Double crossings and dark controls

At p=0.5, threshold 0.35 and gate 12, 99.35% of combined histories would cross both independent thresholds. Averaged over the state scan, that fraction is 55.26%. A first-record latch hides the later crossing by construction. This is why the apparently binary competition must not be mistaken for a microscopic explanation of an exclusive single-photon event. Real semiconductor pixels can share charge, but this model does not calculate charge sharing either.

The combined no-signal control produces one first record in 4,000 histories at the default gate and threshold. That estimate is imprecise, and zero observed dark events in other cases is not proof of a zero rate. At longer gates, stationary classical noise can keep accumulating response. This is a known danger of treating vacuum-like fluctuations as an ordinary energy source.

A passive ground-state absorber does not continually harvest zero-point energy. Correct quantum photodetection involves operator ordering, energy-exchange asymmetry and back-action. A symmetrized/analytic classical noise surrogate does not supply all of those properties. The additive surrogate here also does not derive the field fluctuation and radiation-reaction kernels together. These limitations preclude interpreting its dark counts or outcome fractions as predictions of QED.

Checks and limits of the result

Primary integration uses Δt=0.04, exponential linear-response updates and trapezoidal integrated response. Threshold times are interpolated within a step. A Δt=0.02 check with 2,000 histories at five input values preserves the large non-Born discrepancy; at p=0.25 its first-channel fractions are 0.004 and 0.010 in the two stochastic ensembles. These checks establish qualitative stability, not precision convergence of every probability. The finite spectral grid, cutoff and total period were not exhaustively converged.

The CSV includes 95% pointwise Wilson intervals for first-channel frequencies, all four independent-readout categories, first-record ties and the actual prepared mean population. The worst-case pointwise standard error for 4,000 histories is 0.79 percentage points. Different scan rows reuse noise and are correlated. The supplementary relative-phase and surface-correlation tests also fail to recover a general linear Born curve. No universality claim is made from the finite parameter scan.

What this answers: your suggested sources of microscopic variation can define and move detection basins. But the complex-field sum and a threshold rule do not automatically give those basins Born measures. Here they give strong winner preference, threshold dependence and double crossings. The test rejects this particular additive-noise/integrated-response mechanism as an ideal Born selector; it does not reject all microscopic field–semiconductor mechanisms.

What remains material-specific: choose the semiconductor, photon spectrum, aperture and surface geometry, temperature, operating bias and readout circuit; derive the electronic transitions, field Green tensor and charge dynamics together. These inputs would determine the physical noise scales and prevent choosing them arbitrarily. The present work is a completed exploratory basin calculation, not completion of that material-specific derivation.
