Can vacuum-inspired noise produce Born selection?

Computational investigation · 3 October 2026 · Reproducible seed 20261003

Result: Yes, a deliberately unbiased absorbing process can partition noise histories into basins with Born measures. Three noise distributions reproduce that result. But this success follows from an imposed conservation property in the squared-amplitude coordinate. Neither that property nor the absorbing dynamics has been derived here from QED. Other plausible-looking detector couplings fail, including a Gaussian vacuum-spectrum surrogate.

What was actually tested

The initial state is c₁|1⟩ + c₂|2⟩, with p = |c₁|² and |c₂|² = 1 − p. We tested 41 evenly spaced values of p, including pure states, with 8,000 independent realizations per model and state. Seven models give 2,296,000 primary trials; step-size and memory checks add 600,000. Every trajectory in these experiments resolved. An additional small shared-noise ensemble visualizes basin geometry.

No model selects an outcome by comparing a uniform random number with p. No parameters were fitted to Born frequencies. Noise is sampled independently of the input state. A realization can be understood as an entire noise tape chosen in advance: once the tape and initial detector coordinate are fixed, the outcome is deterministic. The measured frequency is the noise measure of that outcome's basin, rather than its unweighted geometric volume.

Scope: These are dimensionless, phenomenological detector models, not atom–field QED simulations. The principal input is the squared-amplitude coordinate, not a full evolving wavefunction. Phase independence and the choice of measurement basis are assumed in the coupling. The 41-state scan tests population dependence in one basis; it does not independently establish phase invariance, basis covariance, entangled correlations, or a universal measurement theory.

The detector rules, stated explicitly

1. Unbiased absorbing competition

The detector has a competition coordinate x between 0 and 1, initialized to p. The endpoints are absorbing records. At every step:

d = h ξ, with h = 0.07 Δx = sign(d) min(|d|, x, 1 − x) x ← x + Δx Outcome 1 if x reaches 1; outcome 2 if x reaches 0.

We separately use independent unit-variance Gaussian noise, Laplace noise with scale 1/√2, and binary noise ±1 with equal chances. The boundary rule reduces both possible step directions by the same distance-to-boundary limit. This is part of the model, deliberately chosen to avoid the bias of ordinary overshoot clipping. It is not a QED-derived boundary condition or merely a harmless numerical implementation detail.

For symmetric independent noise, each proposed positive increment has an equally likely negative partner. Thus the expected next x, conditional on everything so far, is exactly the current x. This property is called a martingale. With eventual absorption and bounded x, it gives:

E[x at absorption] = x initially = p x at absorption ∈ {0, 1} Therefore P(outcome 1) = p.

This is an analytic result, not a curve fit. The simulations check its implementation. The same argument works for the three tested noise laws regardless of their different jump distributions. For these fixed-step laws, absorption occurs almost surely; there is a positive chance of a sufficiently long run of like-signed, sufficiently large increments from any interior location.

Where the Born assumption could be hiding: initializing a physical intensity or branch-weight coordinate with |c₁|² is not itself a random selection rule. However, choosing that coordinate to be a bounded martingale, with exactly these absorbing endpoints, supplies the crucial probability structure. This is a sufficient construction of Born statistics, not an independent physical explanation of them.

2. Gaussian noise with memory

ξₙ = ρ ξₙ₋₁ + √(1 − ρ²) εₙ; εₙ ~ N(0,1) ρ = 0.9; ξ₀ drawn from its stationary N(0,1) distribution.

The same boundary rule is used. The instantaneous distribution remains Gaussian with zero mean, but the next fluctuation has a nonzero conditional mean given its history. The detector coordinate and noise become correlated, and the martingale argument fails. A memory sweep from ρ = 0 to 0.9 progressively changes basin measures. The sharp near-endpoint behavior belongs to this regulated discrete model; it should not be read as a vacuum prediction. Holding ρ fixed while shrinking h would change the physical correlation time if one assigned a time scale.

3. Nonlinear amplification

d = h ξ + 4(2x − 1)h²

This outward drift amplifies whichever channel is ahead. All other rules match the Gaussian benchmark. In the interior small-step limit it approximates dx = 4(2x−1)dt + dW, with dt = h². Absorption probabilities become more extreme than Born probabilities. Three step sizes show that the discrepancy persists, although the quantitative amplifier curve has boundary-discretization dependence; we do not claim full continuum convergence.

More generally, a scalar Itô diffusion dx = a(x)dt + b(x)dW with absorbing endpoints has an upper-hitting probability u satisfying a u′ + ½b²u″ = 0. Requiring u(x) = x forces a(x) = 0 in this class. This is a statement about this scalar Markov model, not a theorem ruling out all detector mechanisms. A nonlinear coordinate transformation can also introduce Itô drift.

4. Competition in amplitude rather than intensity

x initially = |c₁| / (|c₁| + |c₂|) = √p / (√p + √(1 − p)).

The Gaussian unbiased dynamics is unchanged. Its basin measure equals this initial coordinate, not p. The measured curve agrees with that alternative analytic prediction. Noise symmetry does not determine which function of the quantum state is conserved.

5. Filtered vacuum-spectrum kick and bistable response

A free-space electromagnetic dipole suggests a spectral shape proportional to ω³: mode density contributes ω² and squared vacuum electric-field amplitude contributes ω. We model only a classical Gaussian surrogate for a symmetrized field correlation, adding an explicit cutoff:

S(ω) ∝ ω³ exp(−ω/2) η(t) = Σⱼ √wⱼ [Aⱼ cos(ωⱼt) + Bⱼ sin(ωⱼt)] Aⱼ, Bⱼ independent N(0,1); wⱼ ∝ S(ωⱼ)Δω, Σwⱼ = 1. z′ = −z + η(t), z(0) = 0, T = 3. y(0) = 2p − 1 + 0.6 z(T)/sd[z(T)] y′ = y − y³; outcome 1 is the positive attractor.

There are 96 frequencies from 0.05 to 10 in dimensionless units. The linear filter is integrated exactly mode by mode, using H(ω) = (exp(iωT) − exp(−T))/(1+iω). The bistable equation preserves the sign of y, so classifying its basin requires no time-step approximation. This uses the exact flow's separatrix, not a probability-based draw.

The filtered kick is Gaussian. Therefore the analytic upper-basin measure is Φ((2p−1)/0.6), matching the simulation but not Born. It even produces wrong-channel events at pure-state inputs: about 4.8% analytically, 4.34% measured at p=0. These would be detector errors in an ordinary noisy apparatus, not a derivation of ideal measurement. The cutoff, filter, signal coupling and noise strength are chosen toy parameters. Normalizing by the filtered standard deviation makes this experiment insensitive to spectrum details beyond Gaussianity; it does not establish a special effect of the ω³ spectrum.

Numerical checks and uncertainty

The primary plots show 95% Wilson intervals pointwise, not simultaneous bands. The three unbiased models have goodness-of-fit χ² statistics of 31.92, 56.84, and 39.82 on 39 interior-state degrees of freedom. Their approximate p-values are 0.782, 0.0323, and 0.433. The Laplace realization has a mild uncorrected fluctuation; none rejects at familywise 5% under Holm correction across the three benchmark tests. These p-values do not prove the models: the exact martingale argument supplies the theoretical result.

A separate conservative Hoeffding union bound is ±2.373 percentage points simultaneously for the 205 state/model predictions across the three benchmarks and the two analytic controls. All lie within that bound of their respective analytic predictions. All 50 sensitivity cells and 287 primary cells have zero unresolved trials. Endpoints are retained in the table and plots; the χ² calculation excludes them because the Born variance there is zero. Numerical p-values reported as zero for failed models are floating-point underflow, not literal zero probabilities.

We checked symmetric increments in antithetic pairs, bounded results, absorption counts and the independent analytic predictions for the amplitude-coordinate and filtered-kick models. h = 0.14, 0.07 and 0.035 give the unbiased result within sampling error. The amplifier discrepancy remains. These checks validate the toy experiments and do not validate a physical detector model.

What QED does—and does not—justify

QED and quantum open-system methods motivate field fluctuations, detector back-action, dissipation and frequency-dependent correlations. Quantum noise includes an unsymmetrized spectrum distinguishing absorption from emission. A real classical Gaussian process can reproduce a chosen symmetrized two-point function, but does not reproduce the field commutator or vacuum energy-exchange asymmetry. Arbitrarily combining classical noise with damping is not a complete vacuum bath model; consistent response and fluctuation relations matter. See Clerk et al., quantum noise and measurement.

A simple control makes the limitation concrete: a stochastic diagonal Hamiltonian H(t) = ℏξ(t)σz changes the relative phase in every realization but leaves |c₁|² and |c₂|² unchanged. Averaging can suppress coherence without making an individual trajectory select a branch. More general QED interactions entangle system, detector and environment; decoherence alone is not a single-outcome selection map.

Continuous quantum measurement can produce conditioned stochastic equations with martingale branch weights and Born frequencies. However, standard derivations use measurement operators and their probabilistic interpretation. Reusing those equations would not independently derive Born selection from a classical vacuum draw. See Jacobs and Steck, continuous quantum measurement. Objective collapse theories instead add stochastic modifications to ordinary dynamics; these are additional physical hypotheses, reviewed by Bassi et al.

None of the tested models derives its absorbing rule, conserved coordinate or stochastic state update from a QED Hamiltonian. Gaussian vacuum-inspired perturbations are plausible ingredients for an effective model; Laplace and binary noises are robustness controls, not claims about the free vacuum. The OU-like memory process is generic colored noise, not the electromagnetic vacuum spectrum. The spectral kick is closer in one correlation property but remains a classical surrogate with an imposed detector response.

What this establishes for the proposed investigation

Noise histories can form the requested outcome basins without directly inserting Born probabilities into the final selection rule. However, Born basin measures are not generic: they follow here from conserving the squared-amplitude coordinate in conditional expectation. That is the assumption a microscopic detector theory must explain.

A useful next physical test would specify a system–detector–field Hamiltonian, derive the detector's effective drift, noise covariance and response together, and determine whether the relevant branch coordinate is a martingale without importing measurement probabilities. It would also need to justify stable individual records and test phase/basis dependence. Kuramoto synchronization or a threshold instability could model amplification, but neither by itself fixes the basin measures. The present failures rule out these parameterized toy mechanisms as ideal Born selectors; they do not rule out every QED-inspired stochastic theory.
